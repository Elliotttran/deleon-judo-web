What Is Judo?

柔道 — "The Gentle Way." A martial art, an Olympic sport, and a way of training body and mind.

Origins

Judo was founded in 1882 in Tokyo by Dr. Jigoro Kano, an educator who studied several schools of traditional jujutsu and distilled them into a system that could be practiced safely at full effort. He removed the dangerous strikes and joint attacks, kept the throws, pins, and grappling, and named it judo — "the gentle way" or "the way of yielding."

Kano's school, the Kodokan, remains the world headquarters of judo. Judo became an Olympic sport in 1964 and is practiced in nearly every country today.

The two principles

Seiryoku Zenyo — Maximum Efficiency. Use the least amount of strength necessary. In judo, you don't overpower an opponent — you use timing, balance, and leverage so their own force works against them.

Jita Kyoei — Mutual Welfare and Benefit. You cannot learn judo alone. You need a partner who trusts you, and who you take care of. Kano believed the discipline and consideration learned on the mat should carry into everyday life.

What you'll practice

  • Ukemi — falling and rolling safely. The first thing every student learns.
  • Nage-waza — throwing techniques: hip throws, foot sweeps, shoulder throws, sacrifice throws.
  • Katame-waza — grappling on the ground: pins (osaekomi), and for adults, chokes and arm-locks.
  • Randori — free practice with a resisting partner, the heart of judo training.
  • Kata — prearranged forms that preserve the principles of the art.

There is no punching or kicking in judo. A match is won by a clean throw, a pin, or a submission.

Belts & rank

Judo uses a colored belt system (kyu grades) leading to black belt (dan grades). At DeLeon, rank is earned over time through demonstrated technique, knowledge of Japanese terminology, attendance, and attitude. Black belt ranks progress from Shodan (1st) through Yondan (4th), Godan (5th), and beyond. Our head instructor, Sensei Kaku, is a Rokudan.

Dojo etiquette

We keep the traditions. Here's what they mean.

Bowing (rei)

Students bow when stepping on and off the mat, to instructors, and to partners before and after practice. It's a sign of respect and gratitude, not submission.

Japanese terms

Techniques are called by their Japanese names — o-goshi, o-soto-gari, kesa-gatame — the same names used in every dojo in the world.

Care for your partner

Throw so your partner can land safely. Tap early, and release immediately when your partner taps. Your partner's progress is your progress.

Before and after practicing Judo or engaging in a match, opponents bow to each other. Bowing is an expression of gratitude and respect. In effect, you are thanking your opponent for giving you the opportunity to improve your technique. Dr. Jigoro Kano
