What is judo?

"The gentle way." A martial art, an Olympic sport, and a way of training body and mind.

Founded in Tokyo, 1882.

Dr. Jigoro Kano, an educator, studied several schools of jujutsu and distilled them into a system you could practise safely at full effort.

He removed the dangerous strikes and joint attacks, kept the throws, pins and grappling, and named it judo, "the gentle way" or "the way of yielding." His school, the Kodokan, is still judo's world headquarters.

  1. 1882

    Kano opens the Kodokan in Tokyo.

  2. 1964

    Judo becomes an Olympic sport.

  3. 1970

    DeLeon Judo Club opens in Petaluma.

The two principles

Everything in judo, from a single throw to how you treat a training partner, comes back to these.

Seiryoku Zenyo 精力善用

Maximum efficiency

Use the least strength necessary. You don't overpower an opponent: you use timing, balance and leverage so their own force works against them.

Jita Kyoei 自他共栄

Mutual welfare and benefit

You can't learn judo alone. You need a partner who trusts you, and who you take care of. Kano believed what you learn on the mat should carry into everyday life.

What you'll practise

There's no punching or kicking in judo. A match is won by a clean throw, a pin or a submission.

Ukemi 受身

Falling and rolling safely. The first thing every student learns.

Nage-waza 投技

Throwing techniques: hip throws, foot sweeps, shoulder throws, sacrifice throws.

Katame-waza 固技

Grappling on the ground: pins (osaekomi) and, for adults, chokes and arm-locks.

Randori 乱取

Free practice with a resisting partner, the heart of judo training.

Kata 形

Prearranged forms that keep the principles of the art.

Reigi 礼儀

The etiquette that frames all of it, from the first bow to the last.

How we teach it

Belts & rank

Coloured belts (kyu grades) lead to black belt (dan grades). At DeLeon, rank is earned through technique, Japanese terminology, attendance and attitude.

Dojo etiquette

We keep the traditions. Here's what they mean.

Bowing 礼

Students bow stepping on and off the mat, to instructors, and to partners before and after practice. It's respect and gratitude, not submission.

Japanese terms

Techniques go by their Japanese names, o-goshi, o-soto-gari, kesa-gatame, the same in every dojo in the world.

Care for your partner

Throw so your partner can land safely. Tap early, and let go the moment your partner taps. Their progress is your progress.

Before and after practicing Judo or engaging in a match, opponents bow to each other. Bowing is an expression of gratitude and respect. In effect, you are thanking your opponent for giving you the opportunity to improve your technique. Dr. Jigoro Kano
